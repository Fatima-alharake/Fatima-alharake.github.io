Real-Time Sign Language to Subtitles: Building a Low-Latency ASL Transcription Engine

Translating continuous American Sign Language into live closed-caption subtitles directly on laptop CPUs.

1. What Is This Project About?

Sign language is a complete, natural language with its own complex grammar, spatial syntax, and nuanced facial expressions. Yet, communication between Deaf signers and non-signers remains a persistent barrier, especially in daily interactions, meetings, and video calls.

The goal of this project is to build an end-to-end, real-time American Sign Language (ASL) recognition system that runs directly off a standard laptop webcam and translates continuous signing into live closed-caption subtitles.

Rather than relying on heavy, resource-intensive cloud GPUs or processing raw video frames through 3D Convolutional Neural Networks, the pipeline focuses on low-latency, privacy-first, on-device CPU execution, delivering end-to-end model inference.

2. Architecture: What Models are Used and Why

Why Landmarks Instead of Raw RGB Video?
Feeding raw video pixels (e.g., 224 × 224 × 3 across 64 frames) into a deep video network requires gigabytes of VRAM and high compute, making real-time CPU execution nearly impossible. Furthermore, raw video models overfit easily to background clutter, lighting conditions, skin tones, and clothing.

By extracting skeletal landmarks (coordinates only), we:
  1. Reduce the per-frame input footprint from hundreds of thousands of RGB pixels to just a few dozen floating-point coordinates.
  2. Inherently protect user privacy (no raw video frames need to be retained or transmitted).
  3. Make the model invariant to lighting, and room background.

Step-by-Step Pipeline

1. MediaPipe Holistic Extraction

Google's MediaPipe Holistic is used to isolate the structural points critical to sign linguistics:

  • Upper-Body Pose (15 keypoints): Nose position, shoulders, elbows, wrists, and hips.
  • Both Hands (42 keypoints): All 21 3D joint positions for the left and right hands.
  • Expressive Face Mesh (34 keypoints): Discards redundant 400+ static face points and isolates grammatical anchors, eyebrows (which denote questions and syntactic mood), lips, and eye contours.

2. Coordinate Normalization

Signers stand at varying distances from the webcam and shift around the frame. If left unadjusted, standing closer distorts joint coordinates.

  • Centering: The coordinate origin (0, 0) is shifted to the midpoint between the left and right shoulders.
  • Scaling: All distances are divided by the inter-shoulder distance.

Result: A signer sitting 1 meter away looks identical to the model as a signer sitting 2 meters away.

3. Dropping the MediaPipe Z-Coordinate

While MediaPipe provides (x, y, z) coordinates, normal laptop webcams have no real depth sensors (like LiDAR). Its estimated z-coordinate is an uncalibrated mathematical guess.

  • Through tests on the dataset, it was discovered that keeping the noisy z-coordinate severely harmed model generalization across unseen signers.
  • Stripping out z and operating strictly on calibrated 2D (x, y) planes produced an immediate, measurable jump in test accuracy.

4. Explicit Hand Visibility Flags

When a signer drops a hand out of view, its coordinate defaults to (0, 0). Because the origin is set to the mid-shoulder point, the model could easily confuse "hand absent" with "hand resting on the chest." Binary visibility flags are explicitly appended (is_left_hand_present, is_right_hand_present) to eliminate this ambiguity.

5. Velocity Appending (append_velocity)

Sign language is defined as much by movement dynamics as by static hand shape. Moving a flat hand upward versus downward carries completely different meanings.

Δx = xt - xt-1,   Δy = yt - yt-1

Appending (Δx, Δy) to the static coordinate vector doubles dynamic signal fidelity, making trajectory and speed instantly accessible to the neural network.

6. Motion Energy Gating

One of the bugs in live video classification is resting hand hallucination: when sitting idle or fidgeting, a continuous model still forces a prediction on every frame, generating random nonsense words.

Energy = (1 / 21) Σi=1..21 ||handt[i] - handt-1[i]||

The gate requires at least 2 consecutive active frames above threshold to open, and 8 consecutive idle frames to close. Predictions are suppressed when the signer is at rest.

7. Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) Backbone

Three sequence backbones were benchmarked: Transformers, Bidirectional LSTMs, and TCNs (Temporal Convolutional Networks).

  • Why TCN won: Using stacked 1D dilated convolutions with residual connections, TCN captures long-range temporal dependencies without recurrent bottleneck latency or quadratic self-attention memory overhead.

8. Supervised Contrastive Learning (SupCon) & Metric Retrieval

Instead of only using standard cross-entropy, the model is trained with a dual-loss objective including Supervised Contrastive Loss (SupCon):

  • It actively pulls together embeddings of the same sign performed by different signers while pushing different signs apart in a metric sphere.
  • During inference, queries are compared against an enrolled Sign Retrieval Gallery using cosine similarity. If the similarity score is below 0.50, the system flags the gesture as an Out-of-Vocabulary (OOV) movement or ambient noise, rather than guessing blindly.

9. Live HUD & Debounced Subtitle Accumulator

The live Heads-Up Display (HUD) overlays skeleton tracking, an energy gauge, and candidate prediction bars. A debouncing state machine ensures a sign must be predicted with high confidence across consecutive windows before committing it to the subtitle sentence, preventing rapid word flicker.

3. Dataset Used

The core system is trained and evaluated on the WLASL (World-Level American Sign Language) benchmark:

  • Scale: A diverse 100-gloss benchmark subset representing essential conversational vocabulary.
  • Ingestion: Videos were scraped and aligned via yt-dlp from diverse public hosts and signer sources.
  • Fixed-Length Resampling: Real-world sign clips vary in duration (20 to 100+ frames). Every video sequence is temporally resampled to a uniform T = 64 frames.
  • Data Augmentation: To prevent overfitting on small video counts, the following techniques were applied:
    • Random 2D planar rotation and coordinate shear.
    • Gaussian noise jitter.
    • Temporal speed stretch and compression.
    • Mirroring: Horizontal axis reflection that simultaneously swaps left- and right-hand coordinates and visibility channels, allowing the model to understand signs performed by left- or right-dominant signers.
  • Signer-Independent (Disjoint) Splits: Crucially, clips from the same person never appear in both train and test sets. The model is forced to recognize the sign itself, not the person signing it.

4. How Training and Evaluation Are Done

The Training Pipeline

  • Optimizer & Scheduler: AdamW with cosine learning rate decay and an initial warmup phase.
  • Regularization: Label smoothing (0.1) to prevent overconfident output distributions, paired with high dropout (0.3) suited for small-data regimes.
  • Dual-Head Optimization: The model jointly optimizes classification cross-entropy and contrastive metric alignment via an auxiliary ProjectionHead.

Comprehensive Evaluation Suite

  1. Classification Accuracy: Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy on unseen, signer-disjoint test sets.
  2. Class Balance: Macro F1 score to ensure rare signs are not eclipsed by frequent ones.
  3. Worst-Pair Confusion Analysis: Automatic extraction of sign pairs that the network confuses most frequently (e.g., differentiating signs with identical trajectories but distinct finger curls).
  4. Metric Retrieval Diagnostics: Recall@1, Recall@5, Mean Average Precision (mAP), and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) against the enrolled gallery.
  5. CPU Latency & ONNX Parity: Automated test routines ensure exported ONNX weights match PyTorch CPU outputs within a numerical tolerance of < 10-5.

5. Real-World Challenges & Current Limitations

While the pipeline performs well in controlled tests, real-world deployment on everyday laptops reveals practical problems that are being addressed:

1. Registration Latency & Duplicate Subtitles

The Symptom: Sometimes a sign takes longer to register. The user pauses their hands waiting for the subtitle to show up, and the accumulator ends up printing the same word twice.

The Cause: Heavy system workloads running in the background on a laptop reduce available CPU cycles. When frame rates drop or the rolling window lags, the debouncer's cooldown timer desynchronizes with the user's natural signing cadence.

2. Gesture & Movement Misclassification

The Symptom: The model occasionally mistakes one sign for another.

The Cause: Certain sign pairs share almost identical body trajectories and only differ by subtle finger positions or micro-mouthing on the lips. If lighting is dim or the camera resolution is low, small finger joint nuances disappear, causing the classifier to confuse similar signs.

3. Boundary Misfires (Capturing Before You Actually Start)

The Symptom: The model tries to classify the transition movement while your hands are still rising into position.

The Cause: The transition movement from your lap up to chest level generates high motion energy, occasionally triggering the motion gate a fraction of a second before the actual lexical sign begins.

4. Tracking Drops & Lost Hands

The Symptom: Sudden flickering where skeletal tracking vanishes for a split second.

The Cause: Rapid hand movements cause camera motion blur, and crossing hands in front of each other creates self-occlusions. When MediaPipe temporarily loses track of a hand, coordinates drop to zero, momentarily interrupting sequence consistency.

Conclusion

By combining MediaPipe landmark extraction, coordinate & velocity engineering, Temporal Convolutional Networks, and contrastive metric retrieval, I built an ASL recognition system that runs locally on laptop CPUs.
